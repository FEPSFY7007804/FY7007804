Abstract

  Starship (SpaceX, 2024) is a two-stage launch system made up of the Super Heavy booster and the
  Starship upper stage, both built from stainless steel and powered by Raptor engines that burn liquid
  methane and liquid oxygen. The system is designed to be fully and rapidly reusable, with the long-term
  aim of carrying crew and cargo to Earth orbit, the Moon and Mars. In 2021 NASA selected a Starship
  variant as the Human Landing System for the Artemis programme (NASA, 2024), which gave the vehicle
  a role in lunar exploration as well as in commercial launch. Unlike many earlier programmes,
  development has been carried out through repeated integrated flight tests, in which each flight
  provides data that is used to modify the next vehicle. The progress of these tests is discussed in
  the next section.

Introduction

Analysis and discussion

Starship development progress is the subject of much public documentation such as SpaceX (2024). The key reusability milestone came on Flight 5 on 13 October 2024, when the Super Heavy booster returned to the launch site and was caught by the tower's "chopstick" arms, the first catch of an orbital-class booster (Space.com, 2024). On Flight 6 the catch was aborted after a tower health check failed, so the booster splashed down in the Gulf of Mexico instead. The catch was then repeated on Flight 7 (January 2025) and Flight 8 (March 2025), giving three successful booster catches in four attempts (Next Spaceflight, 2026). The programme later moved to the third-generation "V3" vehicle, first flown on Flight 12 in May 2026. Flight 14 on 28 September 2026 was the first Starship flight to reach orbit, deploying 26 Starlink V3 satellites while the V3 booster made a soft splashdown (CNBC, 2026).

Table 1: Super Heavy booster outcomes on selected Starship integrated flight tests

Integrated flight test Date Booster outcome
Flight 5 13 Oct 2024 Caught by tower (first catch)
Flight 6 19 Nov 2024 Catch aborted, Gulf splashdown
Flight 7 16 Jan 2025 Caught by tower
Flight 8 6 Mar 2025 Caught by tower
Flight 12 22 May 2026 Hard Gulf splashdown (first V3)
Flight 13 24 Jul 2026 Hard Gulf splashdown
Flight 14 28 Sep 2026 Soft splashdown

The upper stage, called Ship, has never been caught. It returns from orbit much faster than the booster, and every Ship so far has ended its flight with an ocean splashdown. SpaceX has announced that Flight 15, targeted for November 2026, will attempt to catch both the Super Heavy booster and Ship 42 with the tower arms at Starbase, which would be the first full recovery of an orbital launch vehicle (Engadget, 2026). Ship 42 has already been lifted into a simulated catch position on the tower, and SpaceX has described success as more likely than not, although the exact date and regulatory approval were still being finalised at the time of writing. If the attempt works, the next goals are to fly a recovered Ship a second time and to complete an in-orbit propellant transfer demonstration, both planned for early 2027 (Northeast Times, 2026).
